### Summary

Kuhn argues that science does not advance only by steady accumulation of facts and theories. Instead, mature science alternates between two modes. In "normal science", a community works within a shared "paradigm": a set of exemplary past achievements, together with shared commitments, that defines which problems count as legitimate and what counts as a solution. Normal science is puzzle-solving within that framework. Over time, anomalies accumulate that the paradigm cannot resolve. These can provoke a crisis and then a scientific revolution, in which a rival paradigm replaces the old one. Kuhn holds that rival paradigms are "incommensurable" in important respects. They differ in standards, problems, and meanings of terms, so choice between them cannot be settled by neutral logic or experiment alone. He compares the shift to a gestalt switch, and he stresses the role of community persuasion and values. Revolutions change how scientists see the world and what they take the field to be. The 1969 Postscript tries to clarify "paradigm" by separating a broader "disciplinary matrix" from narrower "exemplars", and it answers charges of relativism and irrationalism.

### Well-known critiques

- Imre Lakatos (in Lakatos and Musgrave, eds., *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*, 1970) charged that Kuhn makes theory change a matter of "mob psychology" and so irrational. He offered his own methodology of research programmes as a rational alternative.
- Karl Popper (same volume) held that normal science is uncritical and dangerous, and that science advances by continual criticism, not by periods of dogmatic puzzle-solving. Kuhn replied in the volume.
- Margaret Masterman (same volume, "The Nature of a Paradigm") argued that "paradigm" is used in over twenty different senses in the book. Kuhn acknowledged this, and it prompted the Postscript's distinction between disciplinary matrix and exemplar.
