### 2. Summary

A networked improvement community (NIC) is a deliberately organised network of researchers, practitioners, designers and technical experts. It works on one shared, specific problem, such as high failure rates in community college developmental math. A "hub" defines the problem, maintains a shared framework, sets norms and evidentiary standards, and provides technical resources and communication channels. Participants are all "improvers", not researchers studying practitioners.

The method is improvement science, borrowed from fields such as health care (the authors credit the Institute for Healthcare Improvement). The book's preface lists its principles:
- Focus on day-to-day work.
- Run many quick tests of change with iterative refinement. This is the PDSA (plan-do-study-act) cycle, which I know from background knowledge. The excerpt I read did not use the term.
- Treat variation in context and performance as a core design challenge, not noise.
- Use shared measures and a shared theory of the problem (driver diagrams, common measures). Those details come from background knowledge, not the excerpt.

The central claim is that a field can learn to improve faster: schools can "get better at getting better". The mechanism is network-wide learning.

Lineage: a search snippet of the 2011 essay says "Drawing on Englebart (1992), we call this kind of organization a networked improvement community." I did not read the essay and found no "Engelbart" mention in the book excerpt. The link is therefore one snippet-level citation, not a developed lineage. A "bootstrapping" connection was not found in any Bryk-authored text.

Evidence: the book's preface says that about 5% of students in developmental math historically earned college math credit in a year, versus 50% for Statway/Quantway participants. It says this held over three years as the network grew from 27 to more than 50 colleges. Carnegie's 2017 summary reports 50% (Statway) and 64% (Quantway 1, one term) against 16% and 37% for comparison groups. It also reports 43% and 46% transfer rates, against a national baseline of 32% measured over a different window.

### 3. Key concepts

- Networked improvement community: network organised around a specific shared problem and aim.
- Hub: coordinates framework, norms, evidence standards and communication.
- Improvement science: iterative tests of change, practical measurement, and attention to variation.
- Variation as a design problem: understand what works, for whom, under what conditions.
- Practitioners as full participants, not only users of research.
- Colleagueship of expertise: academic, technical and clinical.
- "Getting better at getting better": improving the improvement process itself, at field level.
- Pathways NIC as the exemplar (Statway and Quantway), plus the BTEN teaching-effectiveness network.

### 4. Evidence quality

- The comparison groups differ: the Statway comparison is "16% over two years" on one page, while another snippet gives a 6% baseline. The transfer comparison uses different time windows (5 years vs 6 years). Treat these as descriptive, not causal estimates.

## Relevance to this project (repo-context agent)

1. **Tier**: HIGH. It is the clearest prior, operational naming of a community-level improvement loop (NIC = "networked improvement community", with the "getting better at getting better" slogan), so it bears on the RCI thesis itself even though it says little about verification cost or allocation.

2. **Bearing on the RCI thesis**: BOTH. Support: NICs are a deliberate, human-centered, community-scale improvement loop with no AI self-improvement involved, and the book's slogan ("get better at getting better") is close to "improve the existing loop". It is the nearest prior naming of RCI I know of. Challenge: a NIC is a designed, hub-run organization around one shared problem, with a hub setting norms and evidence standards. That is the opposite of science's distributed, hubless allocation (CLAUDE.md: "allocation is distributed, not decided centrally"). The case that the loop already exists therefore fits NICs less well than it fits science at large. The education evidence is also weak: self-reported by Carnegie, no separation of the NIC structure from the Statway/Quantway curriculum, and mismatched comparison baselines. It is not evidence that improving the loop works. The summary does not report any test of whether the network got better at improving over time.

3. **Loop step(s)**: The loop as a whole, plus norms. Within the loop, steps 3-5 (small iterated tests, shared measures, variation analysis). Trust and allocation are touched only through hub-set evidence standards.

4. **What it gives this project**:
- A worked, non-science, non-AI instance of a deliberate improvement loop, with a hub as the norm-setter.
- Treating variation (what works, for whom, under what conditions) as a design problem, not noise.
- Practitioners as full participants, so users sit inside the loop.
- A named lineage (Engelbart's "bootstrapping" and NIC, via one snippet-level citation). This is possibly a bridge to the bootstrapping and RSI literature, unverified.
