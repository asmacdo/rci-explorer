### Summary

Merton describes the ethos of modern science as a set of institutional values and norms. Scientists are bound by these norms as moral and technical rules, and they are expressed as prescriptions, proscriptions, preferences and permissions. He names four. Universalism means claims are judged by impersonal criteria, not by the race, nationality or status of the person making them. Communism (in the sense of common ownership) means findings are products of social collaboration and belong to the community, so the scientist's claim is recognition and esteem, and secrecy is disfavored. Disinterestedness means institutional pressure to act without private gain, which is supported by peer scrutiny and accountability. Organized skepticism means suspending judgment until facts are checked and applying detached scrutiny to beliefs. Merton argues that these norms are tied to science's role in producing certified knowledge, and that they are reinforced by internalization and sanctions. He frames the paper around threats from totalitarian states that subordinate science to political or national doctrine, and he claims that a democratic order fits the scientific ethos.

### Well-known critiques

- Ian Mitroff (1974), based on interviews with Apollo moon-program scientists, found scientists also express counter-norms such as secrecy, particularism and self-interest. He argued that science runs on competing norms and counter-norms. Michael Mulkay later labeled these solitariness, particularism, interestedness and dogmatism.
- Michael Mulkay (1969, 1976) argued that the norms work as a flexible ideology and vocabulary for justification and boundary-drawing, not as rules that actually govern behavior. This is a sociology-of-knowledge critique. I am citing Mulkay from memory.
- Several authors (Ziman and others) argue that the norms describe an academic ideal and do not describe actual practice, especially in industry-linked or "post-academic" science. Empirical surveys, for example of climate scientists by Bray and von Storch, find the norms endorsed only partly and not fully practiced. Related complaint: Merton's use of "norm" slides between descriptive and prescriptive, and he leaves out technical norms such as logical consistency and empirical evidence.
