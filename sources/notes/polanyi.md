### Summary

Polanyi argues that scientists form a self-governing community that coordinates its work without central direction. Each scientist chooses problems and judges results by what other scientists in neighbouring fields will accept. Because every researcher can see only part of the field, overlapping competence links them into chains of mutual adjustment. This means work can be organised "spontaneously", in a way Polanyi compares to a market, with the community acting like an invisible hand. Scientific standards rest on a shared tradition passed on through apprenticeship. Authority is exercised by respected peers, who rely on three criteria: plausibility, scientific value (accuracy, importance, intrinsic interest) and originality. Polanyi holds that planning science toward practical or social goals would damage it. He says pure research cannot be scheduled, because its payoff cannot be known beforehand. He also holds that science depends on a free society and on a commitment to truth. The essay uses the image of a "republic" of independent, mutually recognising members.

### Well-known critiques

- Philip Mirowski (*The Effortless Economy of Science?*, Duke UP, 2004) argues that Polanyi's use of economic analogies did not succeed. Mirowski also compares Polanyi with Hayek on science and knowledge.
- Ian Jarvie argues that Polanyi's science is a hierarchical, tradition-bound and necessarily conservative community, so it is not a model republic. He applies the same objection to the picture of science put forward by Popper.
- Critics in the sociology of science, especially those who read Polanyi against Merton, say the "free market" picture hides real differences of power, funding and institutions. I am stating this one from background knowledge and have no specific critic to name for it.
