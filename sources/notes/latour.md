### Summary

Latour and Woolgar report on about two years (1975 to 1977) in which Latour observed daily work in Roger Guillemin's neuroendocrinology laboratory at the Salk Institute. They treat the lab as a strange tribe and use ethnographic methods to ask how scientific facts come to exist. Their central claim is that lab work is mostly the production of "inscriptions": traces, graphs, and numbers made by instruments ("inscription devices") and turned into written papers. Statements are passed along a ladder of modalities, in which qualifiers like "it is suggested that" are stripped until a claim becomes a plain fact, and the circumstances of its making are forgotten. They also describe a "credibility cycle", in which researchers invest and convert credit (data, papers, reputation, grants) rather than pursuing truth alone. The case study centers on the characterization of thyrotropin-releasing factor (TRF/TRH). The 1986 edition dropped "social" from the subtitle, because the authors came to see the "social" as an unhelpful category that should not be used as an explanation. The book is often cited as an early source of actor-network theory.

### Well-known critiques

- Karin Knorr-Cetina and other sociologists of science (background knowledge, not verified here) argued that a single lab ethnography, and the claim to treat the observer as an outsider "anthropologist", leave open how far findings generalise and how the observer's own accounts are constructed.
- Critics from the philosophical and realist side (for example Sismondo's discussions in later reviews, from background knowledge) argued that explaining facts as the outcome of negotiation and rhetoric underplays the role of nature or the world in constraining which claims succeed. Latour himself later qualified this stance.
- Japhet Bakuwa (1986, in *Science Studies*/sst.nw.ru) argued that the account will probably not convince researchers who still treat the social and the scientific as two incompatible worlds. Verified at the sst.nw.ru page below, but I only saw the search summary, not the full paper.
