### 2. Summary

The essay asks how the culture of science changes and improves, and how well the "discovery ecosystem" learns. It argues the ecosystem is in near stasis for collectively held social processes such as peer review, funding, hiring and publication norms. It names four bottlenecks:
- control is centralized in a few large funders and institutions;
- no single person or body owns these processes, so changing them is a collective action problem;
- network effects and the "shadow of the future" (fear of later judgment by one's community) homogenize practice, and also pull new institutions back toward the mean;
- successful new institutions have no natural feedback loop that makes them grow.

Part 1 argues that metascience should be an "imaginative design practice" over a vast, mostly unexplored design space. It offers generative heuristics, mainly the "funder as detector-and-predictor" model, which looks for "intellectual dark matter" (important ideas invisible to funders). It also offers many sketched programs. Genovese maritime insurance is the model for a design innovation.

Part 2 asks how outsiders can drive scalable change. The prototype is the replication crisis and "Renaissance" in social psychology. It was driven by decades of ignored warnings, an acute crisis (Stapel, Bem, failed priming replications, the 2015 Open Science Collaboration), new designs (Registered Reports), infrastructure (OSF), and institution-building (the Center for Open Science, Nosek). From this the authors derive the "metascience entrepreneur", a person, often an outsider, who takes responsibility for scalable change. They compare entrepreneurial patterns (arXiv, open-access mandates, Braben's Venture Research Unit). They argue that metascience needs "decisive results" strong enough to convince hostile parties, and that RCT-like designs such as Azoulay et al. are insufficient. They propose outlier case studies, the history of science, and structural diversity as remedies.

They close with a speculative case that transformative improvement is possible. Non-canonical working styles (Kariko, Prasher) are said to be bottlenecked by today's institutions, and culture changes who becomes a scientist. Appendices cover AI and other external factors, a toy model of scale and outliers, and the portfolio construction problem for keeping structural diversity.

### 3. Key concepts

- **Social processes of science**: the institutional practices, incentives and norms widely used in science, such as peer review, hiring and funder risk approaches (Intro).
- **Discovery ecosystem**: the organizations and processes that do basic science, including non-university ones (Intro, fn 1).
- **Metascience as three intertwined elements**: an imaginative design practice, an entrepreneurial discipline, and a research field (Intro, Part 1).
- **Detector-and-predictor model and intellectual dark matter**: a design heuristic in which funders detect hidden signals and predict outcomes (Part 1).
- **Metascience learning loop**: trial, evaluate, scale; scaling is the blocked step (Part 2, "Bottlenecks").
- **Metascience entrepreneur**: an outsider aiming at scalable improvement in collectively held social processes (Part 2, "Metascience entrepreneurship").
- **Decisive result**: a result strong enough to convince someone initially hostile or with a vested interest (Part 2, "Can we use decisive metascientific results...").
- **Metascience accelerator**: a body that takes many ideas through the loop repeatedly and supports entrepreneurs (Part 2).
- **Structural diversity and the portfolio construction problem**: the value of many different working environments, and the open problem of what sets the scale of each (Part 2; Appendix).

### 4. Evidence quality / nature of the argument

- It is a programmatic and conceptual vision essay, not an empirical study. The authors say that "Our arguments unavoidably sometimes use speculative and incomplete reasoning" and that it is "a creative research contribution... a sketch of part of the emerging proto-field". It claims to be a sketch, not a finished theory or a playbook.
- Stasis is supported by illustrative cases, which the authors say are "not meant as dispositive evidence". The cases are the Shanghai Rankings top 10, Kariko, Prasher, quotes from Higgs, Deutsch, Brenner and Klausner, and the roughly 25-year path of funding lotteries. They concede "Our argument is not watertight".
- The most evidence-backed part is the replication-crisis narrative. It cites the OSC 2015 result (36 of 100 replications significant versus 97 of the original studies), five replication-study graphs including Protzko et al. 2020, and more than 300 journals publishing Registered Reports. The authors say this is still a "work in progress".
- The proposed programs are untested "evocations". They explicitly do not debug them or claim any particular one would work. The scale toy model is stated to be "contrived and artificial" (roughly 63% of 1,000 simulations misleadingly favored the control). The authors admit uncertainty about whether metascience can be a science at all (fn 127) and give no good answers to the portfolio problems.
